# The writing standard

Every word on every slide, in every summary, memo and delivery message follows this page. These are defaults. When the person asks for different wording or style, follow them, and keep accuracy, verbatim quotes and confidentiality intact. Correct a violation before delivery. The goal is prose that sounds like a person who chose every word, written for a busy executive.

## Hard bans (search the text for each before delivering)

These forms are banned. They appear here as examples, so the block is fenced.

```
No em dashes, en dashes, or spaced hyphens as punctuation. Use commas, colons, periods, "to" for ranges, "+" for combos. Standard compound hyphens (well-known) are fine.
No negation-reframe ("it was never about X, it was about Y"; "not X but Y").
No fragment stacking (runs of short declaratives used as a drumbeat).
No rule-of-three triads used for rhythm.
No grand closing pronouncements ("this is just the beginning").
No prophecy cadence ("this will feel slow, and then it won't").
No "isn't just X, it's Y" escalation.
No slop vocabulary: best-in-class, seamless, robust, cutting-edge, world-class, game changer, unlock, dive in, delve, journey, landscape (as metaphor), leverage (as verb), utilize, synergy, holistic, impactful, transformative, learnings.
```

## Six rules for plain prose

1. Skip any metaphor, simile or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.
2. Use a short word where a short word will do.
3. If a word can go, cut it.
4. Use the active voice where you can.
5. Use an everyday English word in place of a foreign phrase, a scientific word or jargon, when one exists.
6. Break any of these rules before you write something outright awkward.

Before drafting, answer four questions in order: what you are trying to say, which words will say it, what image or example makes it clearer, and whether you could say it more briefly. Bad prose comes from letting ready-made phrases do the thinking.

## Five habits to scan for

```
1. Dying metaphors: low-hanging fruit, move the needle, north star, table stakes, paradigm shift, at the end of the day, tip of the iceberg. Fix: say the plain thing.
2. Verbal false limbs: utilize (use), leverage (use), facilitate (help), in order to (to), due to the fact that (because), it should be noted that (delete), is able to (can), going forward (delete). Fix: find the buried verb and let it work.
3. Pretentious diction: methodology (method), holistic, ecosystem (unless literal), operationalize, ideate. Fix: write what you would say out loud to a colleague. When the plain version sounds too small, the claim needs work.
4. Meaningless praise words: innovative, powerful, compelling, scalable as praise. Fix: replace the adjective with the fact that earned it, so "a robust pipeline" becomes "the pipeline retries failed batches and alerts after three failures". If no fact earned the praise, cut the claim.
5. Mannered prose: a flourish standing in for a direct statement, even a fresh one. "A dial worth turning" says "a parameter worth varying". "This point earns its keep" says "this point still matters". Such phrases show off the writer, readers can tell, and they carry connotations the writer did not choose. Fix: say what you mean with the literal phrase. This habit needs a judgment call during the read-aloud pass, because no string search finds it.
```

## The editing passes (run on all deliverable prose before review)

1. Meaning: say each paragraph or slide body in one plain sentence. If you cannot, the paragraph does not know what it says either.
2. Cut: throat-clearing openers, doubled modifiers and restatements. Most drafts lose a fifth to a third here.
3. Verbs: hunt passives, name the actor and restore buried verbs.
4. Habit scan: run habits 1 to 4 and the hard bans as literal text searches.
5. Read aloud: rewrite anything you would not say out loud to a colleague, and apply habit 5 here.

## Slide-specific rules

- A headline states the conclusion and never labels the topic. Vary the grammar across the deck, and drop any template repeated slide after slide, such as a repeated antithesis.
- Write the finding the reader will repeat in their own meeting. The ones that travel put a number and its contrast in one line ("down 5% while the rest grew 10%", with its base on the slide per preflight item 15; "from 4.2 to 2.6 days in four weeks"; "five times the district rate"), state a zero with its base ("none of 7,000 tickets mention it"), set a time box, or settle the reader's own hypothesis. They are seldom recommendations. A zero ships with its base and a coverage check (preflight items 21 and 22).
- Body sentences run to about 35 words at most. Check again after any text sweep, because one inserted word can push a long sentence over.
- Use vocabulary the audience uses daily, and leave analyst shorthand out of slide language.
- Use causal wording only where the evidence supports it. Otherwise frame it as a test ("the most promising combination to test") and avoid directives. A prevalence claim that the data fully supports can stay punchy.
- Numbers in prose follow the tier rules in preflight item 16.

## Reader-facing hygiene (anything the audience will see)

- Keep internal tool, pipeline, database and system names out of headlines and body text. Describe the capability ("our usage tagging", "ticket analysis across N regions") and skip the plumbing. The source line is the exception: it names the data system, the population and the window.
- Identify no individual. A quote carries its source, its score or engagement, and its date, with no username or handle.
- Anonymize customer names unless a published reference already names them.
- Translate internal jargon before it ships. Segment predicates become plain descriptions, field names become English, and a term the audience does not use daily gets replaced or defined on first use.

## Readability target

Slide body text and summary prose aim at reading grade 9 to 12: short sentences with one clause doing the work. Scan for offenders mechanically: sentences over about 35 words, comma pile-ups of three or more, and passives. Rewrite them whole instead of trimming mid-clause.

## What editing leaves alone

Verbatim quotes, legal text, names and taxonomy, code, and precise technical terms where the everyday word loses precision. Editing tightens how a fact is said and leaves the fact as it was. If a claim survives only in vague wording, flag it and keep it visible.
